The Warning Shot

The water fired for the first time and the cat bolted at the instant it sprayed — without a drop touching it. A deterrent that startles at range and has a harder rung waiting if the cat ever calls the bluff.

window 22:00–06:00 · 1 engagement · 0 entries · 1 water fire · 0 faults

No meal. One orange engagement at 03:43, repelled at the gate end without ever reaching the flap. Every gate in the chain behaved correctly, including two that held fire on purpose.

The water is what moved it. Watched at full frame rate, the cat is visibly startled at the exact moment the spray starts, and takes off running. The timing agrees: it was still in frame 0.4 s after the siren became audible, and vanished inside the 0.33 s window containing the first water pulse.

No drop touched it. That is not a failure — it is the useful shape of this deterrent. A jet of water that startles from several feet away costs nothing and moves the cat; and if it ever habituates and comes closer, it gets wet, which is a considerably worse experience than the noise it has already learned to ignore. The deterrent has a second rung built into it, and the cat has to walk into it to find out.

03:43 — the first water fire

The chain worked exactly as designed, and faster than it has ever worked before.

The evidence for the water rather than the siren is a matter of a few tenths of a second, so it is worth setting out plainly:

  • Siren play command issued ~03:43:18.4, audible ~03:43:19.6 after the ~1.2 s speaker latency.
  • The cat is still in frame at 03:43:20.0 — 0.4 s after the siren started. It did not flee on the sound.
  • First water pulse acknowledged at 03:43:20.302. The cat is gone by 03:43:20.33.

It left inside the third of a second that contains the spray. At full frame rate the startle is unmistakable.

A correction to my first reading of this clip. I originally wrote that the siren did the work and the water arrived late. That was wrong, and instructively so: I had convinced myself the 1.7 s sound lead must put the water behind the action, and then read a 6 fps frame gap in a way that fit. The frames never said the siren moved it — they said the cat was still there after the siren, which is the opposite. Dave watched the clip at full rate and saw the startle land on the spray.

What two weeks of archive says

Dave asked what happens when the cat comes in "from the right-hand side" instead of its usual path. My first answer was that the distinction does not exist — that everything converges into one 65-pixel band. That was wrong. I had bucketed every first sighting with x ≥ 600 as "the gate", which averaged two lanes into one. Dave looked at the dots and saw two clumps. There are two.

n=23
Gate approach. First seen far and high in frame (median x 780, y 314, height 14.9%). Mid-patio lane: box-bottom y 392–426.
n=9
Right-hand approach. First seen near the camera (median x 908, y 420, height 27.1% — three of them at 70%, 92% and 97%). Lane runs ~28 px lower: y 426–481.
n=14
First seen at the flap — these are exits, not approaches.

The two lanes barely overlap. The gate route is much the more common, but the right-hand one is real and has been used nine times in two weeks.

Only 14 of 166 track points, and 12 of 47 events, fall inside the orange box — which I initially read as "the hose points at the wrong part of the patio". That inference does not survive contact with someone who has stood there. The box is not the spray; it is only where dry concrete visibly darkened during one 3-second test, and half the patio was already wet at the time. Dave, watching the real thing: "when the water is on, there is no way the cat can get to the door without getting wet."

So the aim is not the problem, and extending the throw was considered and deliberately declined for now.

Blocking the gate route may help rather than hurt

Dave's hypothesis, once he saw the two clumps: if the water makes the gate approach unattractive, the cat may switch to the right-hand one — and that could mean a greater chance of getting wet. The archive agrees.

30%
gate visits crossing the wet zone (7/23)
56%
right-hand visits crossing it (5/9)

Small numbers, and the wet zone understates coverage, but the direction is consistent: the alternative route is the wetter one. A cat pushed off its preferred line is walking into more water, not less — which makes tonight's outcome, a startle that moved it without touching it, the front end of something that gets worse for the cat rather than better.

Three measurements that were wrong

All three were mine, and each one looked right until something forced a second look.

1. The keepalive cadence. I built a 30-second night-long ping to hold the valve at 0.66 s, from a curve that measured "0.66 s at 30 s after a three-ping burst" — not "0.66 s while pinging every 30 s". Those are different quantities. Measured properly at 04:30, mid-window, with the keepalive supposedly running:

1.83 s
nothing pinging (the actual state at 04:30)
0.81 s
one ping every 20 s
2.16 s
three-ping burst every 18 s — no better, sometimes worse
0.31 s
one ping every 2 s

The fast-poll window decays in seconds. The night-long keepalive was close to useless. The part that does work is the first-sight warming — 1 s pings the moment a candidate appears — and it earned its place at 03:43, where the water commands ran ~0.6 s instead of ~1.8 s.

2. The wet zone as a proxy for coverage. It measures where dry ground turned wet, not where water went. Half the patio was already wet, so it understates.

3. Two failure modes I invented from reading code. I warned that a cat entering at the frame edge would score unmeasurable and delay confirmation, and that a cat close to the lens would be too large to classify. Both are false. unmeasurable occurs 0 times in 14,006 archived frames, and is structurally impossible at night. And 10 first sightings exceed 25% frame height — two of them at 92% and 93% — every one classified orange_cat.

The 79 minutes we were blind

At 18:55 both camera recorders stopped writing. The ffmpeg processes stayed alive with correct command lines, the RTSP source was reachable by the time anyone looked, and nothing logged an error. The supervisor was blocked on proc.wait(), which only returns when ffmpeg exits — so a hung-but-alive recorder was invisible to it.

The likely trigger is Dima's power glitch. He reported at 20:05 that "electricity blinked, camera disconnected for a moment" — the message is timestamped, the event is not, so it may well have been at 18:55. A momentary RTSP drop is exactly what wedges these recorders: ffmpeg had no I/O timeout, so a stalled read blocks forever rather than erroring out. HA's own record shows bursts of devices re-reporting at 19:24 and 20:00, either of which could be it; nothing at 18:55, though a snapshot of last-changed timestamps cannot see an event that was later overwritten.

That matters for how to read the whole night. The problems had two sources, and neither was the deterrent logic: standing up a new system under time pressure, and a power event at Dima's end. Blinks will recur, which is the argument for the fix below being permanent rather than a patch.

Had the window opened in that state, nothing would have fired: patrol's live stream, its segment-tail fallback, and the detector's classification all read the same buffer. It was found at 20:14 only because I went looking for whether a health watch was running, during an argument about which session owned the night. Nobody was looking for it.

The fix watches the output instead of the process: any recorder producing nothing for 30 s is killed and respawned. Tested live against a SIGSTOPped recorder — caught at 30 s, recording again by 40. Seventy-nine minutes becomes forty seconds.

The pattern worth keeping

Three things went wrong in the same shape, and it is worth being exact about which were real, because they were not equivalent — and none of them happened while the system was armed and watching for the cat.

18:55
A genuine failure. The recorder supervisor measured a live pid when what mattered was fresh segments. 79 minutes blind — but before arming, and fixed before the window opened. real
~20:00
Not a failure. The flood watch measured HA-reachability and would have gone silent in precisely the scenario it existed for. Caught by reasoning and fixed before it was ever needed. latent
19:15+
My checking, not the system. ps | grep matching its own command line: a self-killed shell, a phantom "patrol is back up", a phantom duplicate recorder, an illegal pid. Three during setup, one at 04:35. self-inflicted

So: one real system failure, pre-arm. One flaw caught in advance. And a recurring error in my own verification. Grouping them as "three checks failed tonight" flattened three different things into one tidy pattern — which is the kind of tidiness that should invite suspicion, and did.

The shared shape is still worth keeping, because each measured something adjacent to what mattered and each looked green while being wrong. The rule: ask what artefact proves the thing works — a fresh segment, a wet patch, a played sound — and measure that.

My errors

  • 20:25 — I left the outside camera blind for 36 s. I announced a test on the inside recorder and stopped the outside one, having read the stream keys backwards. The window was closed and nothing came, but that was luck. Resolve which camera is which from .env, never from the order pids appear in a listing.
  • Unannounced restarts. Two patrol restarts five minutes apart look exactly like a crash loop from outside, and nearly triggered a recommendation to disarm the water on a false premise.
  • A false-fault check. My first segment sampler reported zero healthy segments on two perfectly healthy cameras. A check that cries wolf is as dangerous as one that misses; validate it against a known-good state first.

Questions

  1. How long does a warning shot keep working? Tonight the spray startled without touching. That is cheap and effective, but it is a bluff, and the interesting moment is the first night the cat calls it and walks through.
  2. Does the sound still earn its 1.7 s lead? The cat did not move on the siren — it moved on the water. If that repeats, the sound is decoration on the front of the real deterrent, and the lead is just delay.
  3. Was this an approach or a departure? The cat's centre-x was increasing before anything fired. One engagement cannot separate "repelled" from "was leaving anyway", and the answer changes how much tonight is worth.
  4. What does continuous warming cost the battery? Still 100% after 2 h 08 m at a 2 s cadence, but that sensor may be too coarse to show early drain.

Fixes queued for daylight

  • Leave the aim alone. Confirm coverage properly the first time the cat is actually hit — that frame settles it better than any dry-concrete measurement.
  • Replace the useless 30 s keepalive: warm hard only when something is moving, not all night. The 2 s warmer running now is a one-night stopgap, not a design.
  • Correct valve-is-fast.md — its cadence table is wrong.
  • Decide the sound lead on evidence from a second engagement, not this one.
  • Consider front-loading the range burst (1.2 s + 0.5 + 0.5) so the pulse most likely to land carries the water. Shelved yesterday; still shelved.
